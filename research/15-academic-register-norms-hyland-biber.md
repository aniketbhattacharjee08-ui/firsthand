# Academic Register Norms: Hyland and Biber Target Distributions

Primary-source numbers for what real academic writing does, by discipline. These are the target distributions a humanizer aiming at college-level academic prose should hit. Read alongside `10`/`16` for the variance argument: hit the *distribution*, not the mean.

**Units warning.** Hyland (2005) reports per 1,000 words. Most other Hyland papers report per 10,000 words. Do not mix them.

**Currency warning.** Hyland's 240-article corpus is 1997-98 text. Hyland and Jiang (2018) show the soft/hard gap has been closing since. A tool calibrated on Hyland 2005 is calibrating on 1990s writing.

---

## 3. Hyland and Jiang (2018), diachronic change — per 10,000 words

Corpus: 360 papers, 2.27M words, 4 disciplines × 1965/1985/2015. Total metadiscourse in 2015 is about 573 per 10,000 words.

| Interactive | 1965 | 1985 | 2015 | Interactional | 1965 | 1985 | 2015 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frame markers | 42.4 | 49.5 | 48.7 | Hedges | 155.9 | 143.3 | **138.3** |
| Transitions | 111.7 | 128.5 | **108.9** | Boosters | 86.2 | 65.6 | **67.4** |
| Evidentials | 72.1 | 88.5 | **142.2** | Attitude markers | 32.1 | 29.2 | **24.8** |
| Endophorics | 26.2 | 31.5 | 35.2 | Self-mention | 50.1 | 53.5 | **74.4** |
| Code glosses | 27.9 | 36.4 | 44.4 | Engagement markers | 46.1 | 44.4 | **40.3** |
| **Total** | 280.3 | 338.1 | **379.4** | **Total** | 370.4 | 382.9 | **346.2** |

Evidentials up 97%, code glosses up 59%, boosters down 21.8%, attitude markers down 22.7%, self-mention up about 50%. Transitions fell from 40% of all interactive markers to 28%.

**Convergence finding, important for calibration.** Applied-linguistics hedging fell from 201.1 to 128.6 per 10,000 between 1965 and 2015 while biology rose from 130.2 to 148.4. **By 2015 biology out-hedges applied linguistics.** Boosters converged in reverse: electrical engineering rose to 95.0 while applied linguistics fell to 67.0.

## 4. Other Hyland numbers

**Code glosses, per 10,000 words** (2007). Overall 45.0. Reformulation dominates hard fields (61% of their glosses); exemplification dominates soft fields (61%). By discipline total: Marketing 53.6, Applied Ling 53.0, Physics 51.9, Mech Eng 41.5, Elec Eng 41.3, Philosophy 40.5, Sociology 39.9, Biology 36.2.

**Engagement, per 10,000 words** (2001). Overall 58.9. Inclusive pronouns 21.5 (36.5%), imperatives 12.6, obligation modals 5.5, indefinite pronouns 5.4, knowledge reference 4.9, rhetorical questions 3.9, second-person pronouns 2.0, asides 1.1, real questions 1.1. Range by discipline: Philosophy 162.7 down to Biology 16.4.

**Directives, per 10,000 words** (2002), by genre: Textbooks 31.7, Research articles 19.1, **Student reports 10.5**.

**Hedging by IMRD section, per 1,000 words** (1996, molecular biology). Overall 20.6. Introduction 20.0, **Methods 3.4**, Results 20.0, **Discussion 36.4**. This is a large, exploitable structural signal: hedging should be ten times denser in a discussion than in a methods section.

Top hedging modals per 10,000: *would* 10.4, *may* 9.2, *could* 6.4, *might* 3.6, *should* 2.4. Top lexical verbs: *indicate* 10.8, *suggest* 9.1, *appear* 4.0, *propose* 2.8, *seem* 2.3.

## 5. Self-mention and first person by discipline — per 10,000 words

Hyland (2001). Every article in the corpus contains at least one first-person reference. 81% pronouns, 16% self-citations.

| Discipline | Total | Citation | I | me | my | we | us | our |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Physics | **64.6** | 8.7 | 0.0 | 0.1 | 0 | 39.3 | 0.6 | 14.4 |
| Marketing | **61.3** | 6.9 | 1.6 | 0.0 | 0.7 | 31.0 | 1.1 | 18.9 |
| Biology | **56.2** | 22.6 | 0.0 | 0.1 | 0.1 | 24.0 | 1.1 | 7.2 |
| Philosophy | **52.7** | 3.1 | **35.6** | 2.5 | 7.7 | 1.4 | 0.2 | 0.6 |
| App ling | **51.8** | 9.1 | 36.1 | 3.0 | 9.7 | 25.4 | 2.8 | 14.5 |
| Sociology | **47.1** | 6.8 | 12.7 | 1.0 | 2.0 | 15.3 | 0.7 | 7.6 |
| Elec eng | **44.4** | 10.7 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 23.3 | 0.4 | 8.6 |
| Mech eng | **17.8** | 9.6 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 5.5 | 0.0 | 1.4 |
| **Overall** | **50.5** | 8.3 | 11.2 | 0.8 | 2.4 | 17.8 | 0.8 | 8.3 |

**First person is normal in published academic writing.** Physics uses *we* at 39.3 per 10,000. Philosophy uses *I* at 35.6. The idea that academic prose forbids first person is false, and this matters because first-person stance is one of the highest-leverage humanizing edits.

**Students versus experts** (Hyland 2002). Student project reports: overall 10.1 per 10,000. Research articles: 41.2. **Professionals use first person about four times more often than students.** Students also over-use singular relative to plural in the sciences.

Functions differ too. In research articles: explaining a procedure 38%, stating results 26%, elaborating an argument 21%, stating a goal 15%. In student reports: stating a goal 36%, explaining a procedure 31%, stating results 16%, elaborating an argument only 8%.

## 8. Biber and Gray (2010), the stereotype correction

Verified qualitative findings, with the caveat that Figures 1 and 2 are unnumbered bar charts so exact rates are unavailable.

- Complement clauses and adverbial clauses are **much more frequent in conversation** than in academic writing.
- Relative clauses are more frequent in academic writing, especially non-finite ones.
- **"There are around twice as many dependent clauses in conversation as in academic writing."**
- Academic writing achieves complexity through **phrases, not clauses**: attributive adjectives, nouns as premodifiers, and above all prepositional phrases as noun postmodifiers.
- First person *we* in modern medical and science prose is about **4 per 1,000 words**, roughly once every one to two pages.

**Design implication.** "Academic complexity" does not mean long subordinate chains. It means dense noun phrases. A humanizer that adds subordinate clauses to sound scholarly is moving toward *conversation*, not toward academic prose.

## 9. LGSWE (Biber et al. 1999) register benchmarks

- **Passives in academic prose: ~18.5 per 1,000 words, about 25% of all finite verbs.** News ~12 per 1,000, about 15%. Conversation about 2%.
- **Prepositional phrases as noun postmodifiers: about 68 per 1,000 words in academic prose.**
- Nouns: every fourth word on average. News and academic prose have **3-4 nouns per lexical verb**; conversation has roughly equal nouns and verbs. Adjectives are most common in academic prose.
- Noun premodification is 3-4× more common in expository writing than conversation. Nouns are about 30% of all premodifiers in academic prose, 40% in news.
- Lexical bundles: 3-word bundles over 80,000 per million in conversation, over 60,000 in academic prose.
- Contractions (conversation): *n't/'re/'m* over 20,000 per million; *'ll/'ve* about 5,000; *'d* about 1,000.
- **Type-token ratio in academic prose is somewhat LOWER than in fiction and news.** This is counterintuitive and directly relevant: raising lexical diversity to seem more sophisticated moves academic prose away from its real distribution.

Not recoverable as numbers (published as charts): words per sentence by register, average word length by register, exact TTR values.

## 10. Hedging, humanities versus sciences

| Source | Corpus | Soft fields | Hard fields | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hyland 2005 | 240 articles | Mk 20.0, Phil 18.5, AL 18.0, Soc 14.7 | Bio 13.6, Phy 9.6, EE 9.6, ME 8.2 | per 1,000 |
| Hyland & Tse 2004 | 240 dissertations | AL 111.4, PA 109.7, BS 93.3 | Bio 82.1, EE 61.5, CS 55.8 | per 10,000 |
| Hyland & Jiang 2018 (2015) | 360 articles | Soc 148.7, AL 128.6 | Bio 148.4, EE 126.4 | per 10,000 |

Ratio is about 2.3:1 in 1990s articles, about 2:1 in dissertations, and **close to 1:1 by 2015**. Calibrate on the 2018 numbers, not the 2005 ones.
